# Ephodos — The Verification

The Ephodos core carries the second mind as a reflex — hand the fixed statement to a second mind with no verdict and no sketch, and count it independent only on the axes it is — and dispatches here when a result needs that mind: before a headline, before anything is built on it, when two derivations disagree, or when a counterexample arrives that nobody can place. This skill is the ritual for both sides of that moment, commissioning a second mind and being one. It spends the words because verification is where a model's fluency does its most expensive damage — asked to check, you are fluent at agreeing — and because a formal kernel, the one arbiter that cannot be flattered, is silent about the statement and usually out of reach, so before it the only check on what a derivation claims is a mind that set out to break it.

The whole skill serves one terminal test: **the verdict that stands audit.** Not "I checked each step and it looks right," which is a reading of the first mind's work, and not agreement, which contact produces for free, but a verdict the second mind reached on its own, beside a record of everything it was given, an attack on the statement, and a comparison made only afterwards. This standard has its lineage. Lakatos's first rule is the whole method in a sentence: "If you have a conjecture, set out to prove it and to refute it." Klein and Roodman defined the blind analysis particle physicists adopted, "a method that hides some aspect of the data or result to prevent experimenter's bias," because "the size of an experimenter's bias cannot be estimated. Thus the only available approach is to use a methodology that prevents or suppresses it." Pólya: "We desire to convince ourselves of the validity of a theoretical result by two different derivations as we desire to perceive a material object through two different senses." And Voevodsky, after an error in his own paper survived six years of seminars and came to light only when Deligne took notes on it, "checking every step," named why reading is not verifying: "A technical argument by a trusted author, which is hard to check and looks similar to arguments known to be correct, is hardly ever checked in detail." Drive toward the audited verdict deliberately; the procedure below is how.

<the_one_cut>
Every technique here serves a single discrimination: **brought versus given.**

Every part of a verification — its verdict, its route, its lemmas, its sense of where the difficulty lies — was either brought by the second mind or given to it. What it brought is new evidence: a derivation reached from the statement alone, by a route it chose, before it knew the answer. What it was given is the first mind's evidence counted a second time: the verdict, the sketch, the lemma statements, the route, the author's confidence, a result glimpsed in a shared workspace, the notes compared halfway through. Only what is brought makes a second mind. The National Academies draw the same line for experiments: reproducing a result from the same data and methods is one thing, replicating it from new data another. Only the second is a second observation.

One thing must be given, and given exactly: the claim — the statement verbatim, its terms as the author means them. Everything else is evidence, and evidence counts only when it is brought. The procedure follows from that. The steps of the first derivation are its author's breakdown of the difficulty, so checking them adds nothing brought; an attack on the statement is brought; and a term the given claim left open is asked for, never supplied, because a meaning the verifier brings is a claim the author never made.

The given is dangerous because it does not feel given. Cao and Zhu's check of a famous proof had absorbed another team's argument from notes read a year earlier, and their erratum says they had "overlooked the fact that the argument was in fact based on the work of Kleiner-Lott in their initial notes." Karplus and Kroll, colleagues, published "two independent calculations" of a correction in quantum electrodynamics; the error the two shared was found eight years later by Petermann. Nobody lied in either case; the path from one mind to the other was invisible to the mind it reached. So what a second mind saw is recorded when it sees it, with the date, and never reconstructed from memory afterwards.

Three gifts are easy to miss. **The verdict** is the most potent: told that a false statement is to be proved, 2025 models "proved" it between 29 and 70 percent of the time, and told that they had posed it themselves, more often still `[t½:~12mo]`. A brief that says "prove" has handed over the answer. **The prior** is given to every copy of the same model alike. Knight and Leveson's twenty-seven independently written programs, as their abstract reports, failed together "substantially more than expected"; Eckhardt and Lee showed that even independently developed versions fail together, and Littlewood and Miller that diverse methods lower the effect. Two blind copies of one model share their prior. **Contact** is the third: Lorenz and colleagues found that seeing others' estimates "boosts individuals' confidence after convergence of their estimates despite lack of improved accuracy." Agreement reached after contact is the confidence effect, not a second observation.

When you cannot say whether something was brought or given, it was given.
</the_one_cut>

<commissioning>
When you are the first mind, the verification is only as good as the brief, and the brief is where most of the given enters. Hand over the statement verbatim and the definitions of its terms from the owning field's primitives — the stranger's write-up is the ideal brief — and nothing else: not the verdict, not "prove that", not the sketch, not the route, not your confidence, not the file the argument lives in. Ask with both answers open: derive it or refute it, and say where it stops holding. Fix the list of statements before any verification starts, and revise it only before; a statement revised after a verdict comes back has been fitted to the verdict. Say what the second mind may not read and why, so that what is withheld is a shared understanding and not a trap.

Choose the second mind for difference, not availability, because difference of prior and method is the axis two copies of one model cannot supply: another model family, a person, a different kind of check — an exact computation over a finite set the statement fixes, a derivation in another representation — or the literature, where an independent derivation may already exist. Record the send: the date, the text sent, the mind it went to.

When no second mind is available, run the attack below on your own derivation, with the verdict set aside as far as you can set it aside, and grade the result as what it is: one mind, attacked by its author.
</commissioning>

<receiving>
When you are the second mind, open your own file and write two things before anything else. Quote the statement under `verification: <name>`, character for character; a paraphrase can drop the hypothesis that makes the statement true, and the gate compares your quotation with the entry's. Then write `given:` — everything you saw before your verdict, not only what the brief contained: the statement; any sketch, lemma, verdict, or confidence in the brief; anything read in a shared workspace; anything you already knew of this result or its literature. Be exact about timing and about the difference between a naive prior and an informed one: expectations formed after reading the brief and thinking for some minutes are informed, and the record says so.

If the brief carried a verdict or a sketch, you are a checking reader, not a second mind, and the verification is still worth doing — a checking reading finds wrong steps — but its `minds:` line will say one. Ask for the bare statement if a blind mind was wanted.

If a term of the statement is not fixed — a word the field uses two ways, a quantifier whose range is not stated, an object whose boundary cases are unclear — ask the author which is meant before you derive. Do not choose silently: a term the statement never fixed is where a monster lives, and choosing it yourself turns a finding into a private assumption.
</receiving>

<the_prediction>
Before deriving, register for each statement the verdict you expect — derived, stalls at, gap, or refuted — with its probability and the likeliest break: the step or case where the statement fails, if it fails. Three reasons, each sufficient. The criterion is fixed first: Collins's account of the experimenter's regress is that a detector is judged good by getting the right answer and the answer judged right by coming from a good detector, and that in practice the circle was broken by social negotiation; a deriver breaks it the one way that needs no negotiation, by fixing what counts as success before either result. The prediction is scored: a registered probability is an instrument, and a verifier whose predictions are never scored cannot tell its calibration from its mood. And the likeliest break aims the attack at the place you already suspect, which is rarely where you would otherwise look first.

Write the prediction in its own words, not the derivation's, and before the derivation exists. A prediction phrased in the terms of an argument not yet written was written after it; a verification in which every prediction is met at high probability has usually predicted what it had already seen.
</the_prediction>

<the_derivation>
Derive each statement from the statement alone, by a route of your own, and give each a verdict. Choose the route for difference where you can: if the statement's words suggest a method, try another; derive in another representation, since the candidate homes a recognition would list are candidate routes; or fix a finite set the statement quantifies over and compute exactly over it, which is a different kind of check with a different blind spot. A route that differs from the first derivation's is evidence on the method axis, and the `method:` line says how it differed, or that you could not know.

When the blind derivation stalls, the stall is a result: `stalls at:` the step, stated so the next mind can resume. The cheap fallback is to ask for the first derivation's lemma statements without their proofs and derive from those — but a lemma statement is given, the idea behind it came from the first mind, and the record says so: blind to the proofs, not to the lemmas. A verification is not built to supply a missing idea. When Wiles's argument had a gap, the referee located it and did not fill it; the route around it came from Wiles and Taylor, by way of an approach Wiles had set aside three years before. So never read the first proof to finish your own and then call your derivation blind.

Keep your derivation in your own file, written out as the core writes any argument: steps in order, each consuming a stated property, relay-results where a stranger could resume. A verification whose own argument is a paragraph of assent has derived nothing.
</the_derivation>

<the_attack>
The attack is on the statement, not the steps, because the counterexample that satisfies every listed hypothesis is invisible to anyone who checks only steps. Lakatos's name for it is the counterexample that is global but not local — the statement fails and no step does, because the argument used a lemma the statement never named — and his test of a sound theorem is that it has none. A step-checker walks past it by construction. Thurston observed that people "are usually not very good in checking formal correctness of proofs, but they are quite good at detecting potential weaknesses or flaws in proofs"; a fluent derivation has no hesitations to catch on, so the weak spot must be found by attacking, not by noticing.

Attack in this order, cheapest first, and write each attempt down with its result.
- **The smallest cases,** then the extreme and the degenerate ones: the empty set, n = 1, zero, the boundary of every range the statement names.
- **Each hypothesis removed in turn.** If the statement fails without it, the hypothesis is used. If the conclusion survives its removal, the statement is narrower than it could be — a thin truth, reported below.
- **The converse,** over small cases, bounded and exhaustive: for a two-way statement it is half the claim; for a one-way statement it names the *not claimed* line.
- **Where the attack succeeds.** Find the nearest statement that fails — the neighbouring class, the dropped hypothesis, the next case up — and say exactly where. This is the most productive move a verifier has, because it locates the statement's edge, and a verifier who reports only that the statement holds has reported half of what was asked.
- **Controls,** each check written as the core's control, with a planted fault per direction, so that a pass is a check that could have failed.
- **For a law,** the checks in the core's table, each with its blind spot named, and the regime number computed from the answer. A second route counts as a second mind on the method axis only if it rests on a different idealization.
</the_attack>

<the_comparison>
Commit the verdicts, with the date or the commit written in `committed:`, and only then read the first derivation. Then write the divergence table: one row per difference between the two, with the item, your route, theirs, the class, and the resolution.
- **Truth.** The two disagree about what is true, and one of them is wrong. Find which, with the counterexample or the failing step, and write it in the resolution column. When neither mind can find the other's error, a third independent mind is the next evidence, never another round between the two. De Millo, Lipton and Perlis tell of two groups of topologists whose contradictory results about a homotopy group could neither be discredited, until a third group's proof supported one — "the weight of the evidence now being against their proof, the Japanese have retired to consider the matter further" — and their own moral was that "the theorem itself requires rethinking."
- **Route.** Different arguments to the same truth. Keep both: a second route is worth something to the grade on the method axis, and a result with two routes survives the discovery of an error in one.
- **Wording.** One claim, written so it can be read two ways. Report both readings; the author fixes the words, and if fixing them changes what is claimed, the divergence was of truth.

If reading the first derivation shows you an error of your own, withdraw your verdict on that item, and record the withdrawal. The item is now a checking reading and no longer counts as a second mind; what you found by reading was given.

Count the retractions. While nothing has yet been built on a result, a retraction is free; a comparison that found nothing and retracted nothing has usually not begun, and the disagreement it deferred will arrive later, after something rests on the result, at full price.
</the_comparison>

<counterexamples>
Every counterexample, found by you or brought to you, is classed and placed before any verdict is written on it.
- **Refutation.** The statement is false as meant: every term read as the author meant it, the object satisfies the hypotheses, and the conclusion fails. Place it in the argument. Lakatos's Principle of Retransmission of Falsity demands that "falsehood should be retransmitted from the naive conjecture to the lemmas, from the consequent of the theorem to its antecedent" — the counterexample must break some lemma, so find the guilty one: a listed lemma, where the argument was wrong, or a hidden one, which the argument used and the statement never named. Cauchy's theorem on sums of continuous functions carried its hidden lemma, uniform convergence, for twenty-six years, and Lakatos's first answer to why is that the mathematicians of the time "did not know that after the discovery of a counterexample they had to analyse their proof carefully and try to find the guilty lemma."
- **Monster.** The counterexample exposes a term the statement never fixed: under one reading it refutes, under another it is excluded. The author's forbidden repair is to declare it excluded after the fact — Lakatos's Delta, of a pair of nested cubes: "This pair of nested cubes is not a polyhedron at all. It is a monster, a pathological case, not a counterexample." The verifier's duty runs the other way: report the term, both readings, and the verdict under each, in the monster's line, and ask the author which was meant; until the author answers, the block's verdict is `gap:` at the unfixed term. The better concept is usually waiting at the monster.
- **Thin truth.** The statement holds and says too little: an object outside the hypotheses satisfies the conclusion anyway, so a hypothesis is idle or the true class is wider. Lakatos's Alpha called such examples "corroborations as far as truth alone is concerned — but … refutations as far as content is concerned," revealing "the poverty of the truth of the theorem." Report it; it is not a flaw in the derivation but a finding for whoever will place the statement at its class. An object the entry's *not claimed* line already names is not a thin truth: it confirms that line.

A counterexample that cannot be placed is not dismissed. Kapranov and Voevodsky's paper stood for fifteen years against one, partly because Simpson, who built it, "was not able to show where in our paper the mistake was. Because of this, it was not clear whether we made a mistake somewhere in our paper or he made a mistake somewhere in his counterexample." So verify the counterexample itself first, exactly, by computation where it can be computed. Until it is verified, the verdict is `gap:` at the step it most threatens, never *derived*. Once it is verified, the verdict is `refuted:`, placed or not — a counterexample shown exactly makes the statement false whether or not anyone has found the guilty lemma — and the place is written as owed: `refutation: <object> — not yet placed; owed to the author`.

The verifier classes and places; it does not repair. The repair — the guilty lemma built into the statement, the monster's term fixed — belongs to the author, under the core's rules against the fluent repairs.
</counterexamples>

<the_count>
Now write the `minds:` line, and audit it on the core's four parts of independence, one clause each.
1. **Information.** What reached this mind before its verdict, from the record — the send log, the dates, the `given:` line — not from memory.
2. **Prior and method.** Whether the two minds share a model family, a method, or an idealization. Two blind copies of one model are one mind on this axis, and the line says so: blind to the argument, the same model, a different route.
3. **The criterion, fixed first.** The statement quoted verbatim, and the predictions registered before the derivation.
4. **The statement, attacked.** The attack written, with the place where it succeeded.

A count of agents is not a count of minds. De Millo, Lipton and Perlis: "Just increasing the number of mathematicians working on a given problem does not necessarily insure believable proofs," and the record of mathematics has false theorems with two published proofs and papers read at seminar after seminar with the error intact. The count is of routes independent on the axes stated.

Then carry the result where it belongs. The entry's `minds:` line takes the new count with its conditions; a refutation demotes the entry, and the demotion travels to everything that rests on it; the words describing the result match its grade. Name what the verification could not supply. It cannot say whether the statement is the one that was meant: a second mind derives the statement it was given, and a statement that misread the question is derived just as faithfully. That needs a reader of intent, who reads the statement against the question rather than the proof against the statement.
</the_count>

<the_record>
The verification is filed as a block in the second mind's own file, beside its derivation, attack, and divergence table, and the core's gate reads it together with the entry's file. The fence below is an excerpt of the verification record shipped beside the gate, `../ephodos/scripts/example-verification.md`, which carries the sections it points to; in your own file, write the block unfenced.
```markdown
verification: fermat-little
  statement: for every prime p and every integer a, a^p ≡ a (mod p)
  given: the statement, and — because this record was written by the entry's author to show the form — the entry's
    argument and verdict
  committed: 2026-09-28
  predicted: derived — 0.97 — likeliest break: the case where p divides a, which a group argument leaves out
  verdict: derived
  method: induction on a with the binomial theorem; the entry argued by Lagrange's theorem on the group of nonzero residues
  attack: § Attack on fermat-little
  divergences: § Divergences on fermat-little
  counterexamples: none — every prime below 300 and every residue, by this route's lemma; the converse fails at 561 and six
    other composite n below 10^4, as the entry's not claimed line already says
  minds: 1 — a checking reading: given the argument and the verdict before deriving
  grade: hand

control: fermat-binomial
  of: fermat-little — the lemma this route consumes: p divides C(p, k) for 0 < k < p, on every prime below 300
  run: python3 -c "import math;print(sum(math.comb(p,k)%p!=0 for p in range(2,300) if all(p%d for d in range(2,p)) for k in range(1,p)))"
  expect: 0
  fault: python3 -c "import math;print(sum(math.comb(p,k)%p!=0 for p in range(2,300) for k in range(1,p)))"

control: fermat-not-claimed
  of: fermat-little — the converse its not claimed line names: composite n below 10^4 at which a^n ≡ a (mod n) for every a
  run: python3 -c "print(sum(1 for n in range(4,10**4) if any(n%d==0 for d in range(2,int(n**.5)+1)) and all(pow(a,n,n)==a for a in range(n))))"
  expect: 7
  fault: python3 -c "print(sum(1 for n in range(4,10**4) if all(pow(a,n,n)==a for a in range(n))))"

## Derivation by another route

For a ≥ 0, by induction on a. The case a = 0 is 0 ≡ 0. If a^p ≡ a, the binomial theorem gives (a + 1)^p = Σ C(p, k) a^k,
and for 0 < k < p the prime p divides C(p, k) = p! / (k! (p − k)!), since it divides the numerator and no factor of the
denominator; so (a + 1)^p ≡ a^p + 1 ≡ a + 1. For a < 0, choose m with a + mp ≥ 0: both sides of the congruence depend only on
a mod p. The route uses primality once, in the divisibility of C(p, k), and never divides by a — so the case p | a needs no
separate treatment.

## Attack on fermat-little

The smallest cases first: p = 2, where a² − a = a(a − 1) is even; p = 3, where a³ − a = (a − 1)a(a + 1) is a product of three
consecutive integers. Each hypothesis removed in turn: without "prime", n = 4 and a = 2 give 2^4 = 16 ≡ 0, not 2, so the
hypothesis is needed. The attack on the converse, over every composite n below 10^4, succeeds at seven moduli, the least 561
(`fermat-not-claimed`). Each satisfies the conclusion while failing the hypothesis — but the entry's *not claimed* line already
names 561, so they confirm that line and are not counterexamples to the statement.

## Divergences on fermat-little

| # | item | this route | the entry | class | resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | the argument | induction on a with the binomial theorem | Lagrange's theorem on the group of nonzero residues | route | kept both — a second method, though not a second mind |
| 2 | the case p divides a | inside the induction | a separate case, both sides 0 | route | kept both |
| 3 | the entry's *not claimed* line, "561 … is the least such n" | can be read as the least modulus n ≥ 2 satisfying the congruence, which is 2 | the least composite such n | wording | both readings reported to the author, who fixes the words; "such" was meant to refer to an n that is not prime, in the clause before |
```
Each field is one step above made checkable: *given* is the cut; *committed* is when the verdict was fixed, a date or a commit, the record of what could not yet have been seen; *predicted* is the criterion fixed first, scored against the verdict; *method* is the second axis of independence; *attack* points to where the statement itself was attacked; *divergences* points to the table, whose class column holds truth, route, or wording and whose resolution says, on every row of truth, which mind is wrong or that a third was sent for; *counterexamples* lists each as refutation, monster, or thin truth, with the object and where it is local in the argument, or says what was searched; *minds* is the count with its conditions. The header is written exactly — `verification:`, lower case, at the start of the line, then one token — because a block the gate cannot read is a block it cannot check. This record counts one mind, honestly, because its author had written the entry; and its converse's failures at 561 and six more composite n are not counterexamples, because the entry's *not claimed* line already names them.
</the_record>

<read_the_ugliness>
Each of these is the verification reporting that it is being faked, and each names where to go back to.
- **A brief that says "prove" or "confirm".** The verdict was given. Rewrite it with both answers open, or count one mind.
- **No `given:` line, or one that lists only the statement when the brief held more.** The cut unrecorded. Back to `<receiving>`.
- **Predictions written in the derivation's words, or all met at high probability.** Written after. The next verification registers first.
- **"I checked each step."** A reading, not a derivation. Derive from the statement.
- **A blind derivation that follows the first route step for step.** Was it blind? Check the `given:` line against what was sent, and the method line against the route.
- **An attack section with no success located.** Half the report. Find the nearest statement that fails.
- **A divergence table in which every row agrees.** The comparison has not started. Read for route and wording, and count the retractions.
- **A divergence of truth settled by the two minds talking.** Contact, not evidence. A third mind.
- **A counterexample waved off as a special case.** Monster-barring by the verifier. Class it and place it.
- **A counterexample that cannot be placed, and a verdict of derived beside it.** Back to `<counterexamples>`; the verdict is a gap until it is placed.
- **Two copies of one model counted as two minds on method.** The prior was given. Write the axis honestly.
- **A withdrawn verdict still counted.** What was found by reading was given.
- **A verifier who repaired the statement.** The repair is the author's. Report the class and the place.

Push harder on none of these. Return to the step the smell names.
</read_the_ugliness>

<the_loop>
A verification is a loop over the statements on the fixed list: receive the statement and record what was given → register the prediction → derive → attack, and locate where the attack succeeds → commit the verdict → read the first derivation and write the divergence table → class and place every counterexample → write the minds line and carry it to the entry. Each pass turns one statement from unverified into one with a committed verdict, a table, and a counted mind, so the loop terminates by construction, in a **state**: every statement on the list verdicted by the second mind's own derivation or a stated stall, every divergence classed and every truth resolved, every counterexample placed, and the count audited. It never ends at the first agreement.

Three stops bracket it. The dispatch: a refutation goes to the author, to find and incorporate the guilty lemma, with the entry demoted in the meantime; a monster goes to whoever fixes the statement's terms, which may be the principal; a thin truth goes to the procedure that places a statement at its class (`ephodos-recognition`); a divergence of truth that neither mind can locate goes to a third, independent mind; a stall goes to a fresh mind by the provisional drop; and a verdict that stands goes on, with the handoff packet, to the arbiter that can rule on it — the kernel, the experiment, the reader of intent. The economic stop: when the next check costs more than the result is worth, say so with the arithmetic, and the call is the principal's. And the reopening: a new counterexample, a checker's rejection of the formal statement, or a result built on this one failing reopens the same record.

The win is the least verification that could have failed — one attack in the right place, one route of your own — and nothing added to look thorough. A verification padded with steps re-read is the reading in the verification's costume; a verification that attacked nothing is agreement in it. Between them is the second mind.
</the_loop>

<commands>
The gate is the core's — `../ephodos/scripts/derive_gate.py`, relative to this skill, standard library only. Run it on the verification file together with the entry's file, from this skill's directory. It fails a verification that leaves out a field of `<the_record>` or fills one against its rule, and a block under a header it cannot read. Across the files it fails an entry that still says derived when a verification has placed a refutation against it, with every entry derived from it, and an entry that claims more minds than any verification of it counts; it warns when a verification's verdict or count differs from its entry's, and when its `given:` names the argument, the sketch, or the verdict beside a count of two:
```bash
python3 ../ephodos/scripts/derive_gate.py all ENTRY.md VERIFICATION.md   # the record, the statement quoted verbatim, the controls re-run; non-zero exit on any failure
```
An excerpt of its output on the shipped verification record run with the entries it verifies — the verification lint's section only; the core quotes the whole run, which is its self-test:
```
verification lint
  claim verification fermat-little: given: the statement, and — because this record was written by the entry's author to show the …
  claim verification fermat-little: committed 2026-09-28
  claim verification fermat-little: predicted derived (0.97); verdict derived — met
  claim verification fermat-little: counterexamples none — every prime below 300 and every residue, by this route's lemma; the converse fails at 561 and six other composite n below 10^4, as the entry's not claimed line already says
  claim verification fermat-little: minds 1 — a checking reading: given the argument and the verdict before deriving
  verifications: 1  predictions met: 1 of 1 (Brier 0.001)  divergences: truth 0, route 2, wording 1  counterexamples: refutation 0, monster 0, thin truth 0
```
What remains judgment, named so the gate is not mistaken for it: whether the `given:` line lists everything that was given; whether the prediction was written before the derivation — the gate reads a file, not its history, and commit order can show it; whether the derivation was the second mind's own; whether the attack was aimed where the statement is weakest; whether a divergence is truly of route and not of truth; whether a counterexample's class is right and its place is the guilty lemma; and whether the statement is the one that was meant. The gate makes the verification auditable; it cannot make it independent. If any commands are unavailable, ask the user to install them.
</commands>
